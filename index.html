Abstract

Actuator degradation turns quadruped locomotion into a coordination problem requiring joints to compensate for lost actuation. Prior work suggests that morphology-aware graph policies improve learning and generalization under body perturbations. We ask whether these benefits can be strengthened by explicitly modeling higher-order mechanical structure. We represent the Unitree Go1 as a cell complex with limb- and body-level rank-2 cells and apply Hodge-based message passing. Under degradation training, the node-edge-face Hodge actor achieves the highest return on unseen actuator degradations, with higher survival and lower velocity-tracking error. These results support higher-order morphology as a useful inductive bias for whole-body compensation under actuator degradation.

Higher-Order Go1 Morphology & Multi-Rank Hodge Propagation

Fig. 1: Go1 higher-order morphology. Twelve actuated joints form 0-cells; sixteen 1-cells give eight serial couplings, four around the hip ring, and four dashed virtual closures bounding each leg face. Five shaded 2-cells encode four limb structures plus the coplanar hip body cell.

Most morphology-aware policies represent a quadruped as a classical graph of joints (0-cells) and links (1-cells). At a higher level, however, joints form persistent mechanical units: each three-joint leg acts jointly to place a foot, and the four hips form a rigid coplanar ring. We promote these closed cycles to explicit rank-2 cells (faces), yielding a cell complex with $|\mathcal{X}_0|=12$ nodes, $|\mathcal{X}_1|=16$ edges, and $|\mathcal{X}_2|=5$ faces coupled by oriented incidence matrices $B_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{12 \times 16}$ and $B_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{16 \times 5}$:

$$H_0 \;\overset{B_1^\top}{\underset{B_1}{\rightleftarrows}}\; H_1 \;\overset{B_2^\top}{\underset{B_2}{\rightleftarrows}}\; H_2.$$

At each layer $\ell$ and rank $k \in \{0, 1, 2\}$, the actor combines same-rank Hodge Laplacian propagation $S_k$ with cross-rank incidence messages $C_k$:

$$L_k = \underbrace{B_k^\top B_k}_{L_k^{\mathrm{lower}}} + \underbrace{B_{k+1} B_{k+1}^\top}_{L_k^{\mathrm{upper}}}, \qquad S_k = L_k H_k,$$ $$C_k = \underbrace{B_k^\top H_{k-1}}_{\text{from } k-1} + \underbrace{B_{k+1} H_{k+1}}_{\text{from } k+1},$$ $$M_k^{(\ell)} = \alpha_k^{(\ell)} S_k^{(\ell)} + \bigl(1 - \alpha_k^{(\ell)}\bigr) C_k^{(\ell)},$$ $$H_k^{(\ell+1)} = H_k^{(\ell)} + \operatorname{RMSNorm}\!\left(g_k^{(\ell)}\bigl(M_k^{(\ell)}\bigr)\right).$$

All five compared actors (MLP-C, MLP-D, GCN, Hodge-L over ranks 0–1, and Hodge-F over ranks 0–2) are capacity-matched within 0.5% of 171,296 parameters and share an identical privileged PPO critic $V_\phi(o_t^{\mathrm{priv}})$ and 400M-step training budget across seeds $\{11, 12, 13\}$.

Experimental Results

We model degradation of actuator $i$ by scaling its nominal torque limit $\tau_i^{\mathrm{max}}(\rho_i) = \rho_i \tau_i^{\mathrm{max,nom}}$ with $\rho_i \in [0, 1]$, and evaluate policies across two training and evaluation regimes on the MuJoCo Playground Go1JoystickFlatTerrain task: the Healthy regime ($H$) with $\rho_i = 1$ and the Degraded regime ($D$) where two actuators are degraded at a time across $\binom{12}{2} = 66$ unordered pairs (split into 44 training and 22 held-out pairs).
